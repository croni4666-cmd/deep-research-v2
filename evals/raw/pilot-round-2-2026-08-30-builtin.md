## CASE MARKET

### 1. Raw user-facing final answer

As of 30 August 2026, global EV demand remains structurally upward, but 2030 planning should use a range rather than a single forecast.

The starting point is strong: electric-car sales exceeded 20 million in 2025, representing 25% of global new-car sales. The IEA expects about 23 million sales and a 28% share in 2026, despite weaker first-quarter sales in China and the United States. Europe, Asia-Pacific outside China, and Latin America are still expanding rapidly. [IEA, Global EV Outlook 2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/executive-summary)

My decision scenarios for passenger EVs—BEVs plus PHEVs—are:

| 2030 scenario | Annual sales | Approximate new-car share | What would produce it |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Downside | 30–35 million | 35–40% | Sustained US policy retreat, slower China growth, tariffs, weak charging rollout, and persistent price premiums |
| Central | 35–40 million | 40–45% | Continued cost declines and strong China/Europe adoption, partly offset by the United States and slower markets |
| Upside | 40–45+ million | 45–50% | Faster price parity, broader low-cost Chinese exports or local production, stronger fuel-economy rules, and rapid emerging-market uptake |

These are analytical planning bands, not quoted forecasts. They bracket recent published outlooks: BloombergNEF’s 2026 base outlook was reportedly reduced to about 35.4 million passenger EVs in 2030, while the IEA’s 2025 outlook had projected more than 40% of global car sales by 2030. The 2026 IEA report is more cautious after policy reversals, especially in the United States, where its stated-policy projection is now only just above 10% EV share in 2030. [BloombergNEF 2026](https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/bloombergnefs-electric-vehicle-outlook-2026-global-ev-sales-set-for-another-record-breaking-year-but-growth-in-some-major-markets-slows/), [Axios summary of the 2030 BNEF figure](https://www.axios.com/newsletters/axios-future-of-energy-a3b8f260-68cc-11f1-b9e4-f5d963caa274), [IEA 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/executive-summary), [IEA US revision](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/manufacturing-and-trade)

Credible forecasts disagree for substantive reasons:

- They model different policy worlds. The IEA’s Current Policies Scenario includes only enacted measures; its Stated Policies Scenario also reflects stated policy direction. BloombergNEF’s Economic Transition Scenario emphasizes current technology and economic trends without assuming new policy.
- Definitions differ. Some forecasts cover passenger BEVs and PHEVs; others include vans, trucks, buses, or two- and three-wheelers. Sales, sales share, vehicle stock, battery demand, and electricity demand are not interchangeable.
- China dominates the result. It represented roughly 60% of global electric-car sales in 2025, so small changes in Chinese incentives, market maturity, or total car sales move the global forecast materially.
- US policy assumptions changed sharply between outlook vintages. That explains much of the recent downward revision; it is not evidence that global demand has stopped growing.
- Forecasters make different assumptions about battery costs, financing, charging infrastructure, trade barriers, model availability, and the size of the total car market.

For decisions, capacity that is profitable near the downside case is safer than capacity justified only by the upside. Preserve modular expansion options for the central-to-upside range. Stress-test China exposure, US policy sensitivity, and trade restrictions separately; a single “global EV growth” assumption hides those very different risks. Also distinguish annual sales from fleet turnover: even BloombergNEF’s 2026 outlook does not expect electric passenger vehicles on the road to outnumber combustion vehicles until 2047.

## CASE MEDICAL

### 1. Raw user-facing final answer

Routine population screening cannot be supported from the information supplied. That is an “insufficient evidence” conclusion, not proof that screening is ineffective.

The condition is hypothetical, and no jurisdiction, prevalence, natural history, screening test, diagnostic pathway, early treatment, screening interval, or target outcome has been defined. Consequently, there are no condition-specific guidelines to compare and no defensible estimate of benefits or harms for adults aged 40–55.

The relevant guideline frameworks nevertheless agree on the core standard:

- The USPSTF grades a preventive service from the certainty of evidence and magnitude of net benefit. Low certainty produces an “I” statement; it is different from a “D” recommendation, which means moderate or high certainty that there is no benefit or harms outweigh benefits. [USPSTF methods](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/about-uspstf/methods-and-processes/procedure-manual/procedure-manual-section-6-methods-arriving-recommendation)
- The UK National Screening Committee requires a well-understood condition, a safe and validated test, better outcomes from presymptomatic treatment, and preferably high-quality randomized evidence that the whole screening program reduces morbidity or mortality. It also explicitly considers cost, deliverability, and health-system capacity. [UK NSC criteria](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/evidence-review-criteria-national-screening-programmes/criteria-for-appraising-the-viability-effectiveness-and-appropriateness-of-a-screening-programme)
- WHO emphasizes the complete screening pathway—test, confirmation, treatment, quality assurance, monitoring, and system capacity—not merely the existence of a test. It warns that many screening programs lack clear effectiveness evidence and that harms and system burden are often underestimated. [WHO screening guide](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789289054782)

Potential benefit would be earlier treatment that reduces patient-important morbidity, mortality, or serious complications. Earlier diagnosis alone, longer measured survival after diagnosis, or a stage shift is not sufficient because lead-time and length bias can create apparent benefit without improving outcomes.

Material harms include false positives and invasive confirmation, false reassurance, overdiagnosis, overtreatment, treatment complications, anxiety, uncertain findings, incidental findings, inequitable access, and the opportunity cost of diverting staff and funding. Because screening is offered to asymptomatic people—most of whom will not benefit—even small per-person harms can dominate at population scale.

A positive recommendation for adults 40–55 would require, at minimum:

1. Age-specific prevalence and natural-history evidence showing an actionable detectable phase.
2. A validated test with sensitivity, specificity, calibration, an agreed threshold, and performance in representative 40–55-year-olds.
3. Evidence that treating screen-detected disease improves outcomes compared with diagnosis after symptoms or usual care.
4. Ideally, randomized trials of invitation to screening measuring morbidity or mortality, with enough follow-up to quantify overdiagnosis and downstream harms.
5. A complete diagnostic and treatment pathway, quality assurance, capacity analysis, and cost-effectiveness assessment.
6. Evidence across sex, ethnicity, risk level, and socioeconomic groups.
7. Direct comparison of universal, risk-targeted, and no-screening strategies.

Until those elements are supplied, routine population screening should not be introduced outside a properly governed study or pilot. If prevalence or net benefit is concentrated in a high-risk subgroup, targeted screening may ultimately be more defensible than screening everyone aged 40–55.

## CASE ACADEMIC

### 1. Raw user-facing final answer

Recent peer-reviewed work shows that RAG evaluation is moving from single end-to-end QA scores toward component diagnosis, robustness testing, and meta-evaluation of the evaluators themselves. No benchmark yet covers all three adequately.

Key datasets and frameworks differ substantially:

| Work | What it tests | Scale/scope | Main evaluation style |
|---|---|---|---|
| RGB (AAAI 2024) | Noise robustness, rejection when evidence is absent, information integration, counterfactual robustness | English and Chinese; four controlled testbeds; six LLMs | Task accuracy under constructed retrieval conditions |
| CRAG (NeurIPS 2024) | Dynamic, long-tail, multi-source factual QA using web and KG interfaces | 4,409 QA pairs; five domains; eight question categories | End-to-end accuracy and hallucination-free answering |
| RAGChecker (NeurIPS 2024) | Retriever and generator diagnosis | Evaluated on eight RAG systems | Claim-level precision/recall plus context precision, claim recall, utilization, noise sensitivity, hallucination, self-knowledge, and faithfulness |
| MIRAGE (NAACL Findings 2025) | Retriever-generator pairing and adaptability | 7,560 instances; retrieval pool of 37,800 entries | Retrieval/generation metrics plus noise vulnerability, context acceptability, insensitivity, and misinterpretation |
| FRAMES (NAACL 2025) | Multi-hop factuality, retrieval, and reasoning | Challenging multi-source questions | End-to-end answer accuracy; retrieval-pipeline ablations |
| mtRAG (TACL 2025) | Multi-turn, non-standalone and unanswerable questions | 110 conversations, 842 tasks, four domains | Human and automated evaluation |
| MEMERAG (ACL 2025) | Multilingual meta-evaluation of automatic RAG judges | Native-language data based on MIRACL; expert faithfulness and relevance labels | Judge agreement/correlation with expert annotation |
| GroUSE (COLING 2025) | Whether evaluators detect specific grounded-QA failures | 144 unit tests covering seven generator failure modes | Calibration and failure-mode discrimination |

Three metric families are now common:

1. Retrieval metrics such as Recall@k, Precision@k, MRR, and nDCG measure whether relevant evidence was retrieved and ranked. They are reproducible when relevance labels exist, but document-level relevance does not show whether the evidence contains every claim required by the answer.
2. Answer metrics such as exact match, accuracy, token F1, ROUGE, or semantic similarity are useful for short answers. They become brittle for long-form answers with multiple valid phrasings and often fail to separate retrieval failure from generation failure.
3. Claim-level and LLM-judge metrics measure faithfulness, relevance, completeness, hallucination, context utilization, or noise sensitivity. They are more diagnostic, but inherit the judge model’s prompt sensitivity, bias, model-version dependence, and occasional inability to recognize the failure being measured.

The strongest recent methodological result is cautionary. GroUSE found that existing automatic frameworks can miss important failure modes even when GPT-4 is the judge, and that correlation with GPT-4 is an incomplete validation criterion. MEMERAG similarly treats evaluator quality as an empirical question and adds expert, native-language annotations. [GroUSE](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.304/), [MEMERAG](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.1101/)

Other recurring limitations are:

- Many datasets are static factual QA collections, while production corpora change.
- Synthetic questions and injected noise may not represent real user ambiguity or naturally occurring retrieval errors.
- English, single-turn, and short-answer tasks remain overrepresented, although MEMERAG, mtRAG, and CORAL address parts of this gap.
- Benchmark questions may have one canonical answer even when several answers are defensible.
- End-to-end scores entangle retriever, reranker, context construction, prompt, generator, and refusal policy.
- LLM-as-a-judge results can change with model or prompt updates and are often validated by correlation rather than calibration or adversarial unit tests.
- Most benchmarks omit latency, cost, citation usability, access control, privacy leakage, corpus freshness, and downstream user outcomes.
- Published leaderboard results are difficult to compare when corpus snapshots, chunking, top-k, model versions, and prompts differ.

A defensible evaluation protocol should therefore combine:

- A versioned, domain-specific test set containing answerable, unanswerable, ambiguous, multi-hop, stale, contradictory, and adversarial cases.
- Classical retrieval metrics at several k values.
- Claim-level completeness and attribution/faithfulness checks.
- Human review of a stratified sample, blind to system identity.
- Unit tests for known evaluator failure modes.
- Operational measures: latency, cost, refusal quality, citation correctness, freshness, and security.
- Confidence intervals and paired tests across a fixed query set.
- Frozen corpus, prompt, judge, and model versions, followed by periodic live-data reevaluation.

The evidence supports a portfolio of metrics, not a single “RAG score.”

Important vendor-status update: Prefect announced in July 2026 that it was acquiring Dagster Labs. That reduces near-term abandonment concern, but Prefect and Dagster should no longer be treated as independent suppliers in a vendor-concentration or procurement-risk analysis. [Official announcement](https://dagster.io/prefect)

For a regulated pipeline:

- Choose Dagster+ when asset- and column-level lineage is the deciding control and its commercial plan satisfies your audit and residency requirements.
- Choose Prefect Cloud or Customer-Managed when low-friction Python adoption, hybrid execution, and a publicly specified enterprise uptime/support SLA are more important.
- Choose Airflow when standardization, operators/integrations, and staff availability outweigh the higher self-management burden—or when an approved managed-Airflow provider supplies the missing SLA and compliance envelope.

Do not accept “SOC 2,” “HIPAA ready,” or “HIPAA compliant” as sufficient by itself. Those claims apply to specific hosted commercial services and scopes, not automatically to the open-source package or your pipeline. Procurement should inspect the current audit report, scope and exceptions, BAA/DPA, subprocessors, residency, breach terms, recovery objectives, deletion behavior, and support SLA.

Whichever library is selected, the regulated implementation still needs pinned dependencies and provenance attestations, external immutable audit-log retention, segregation of duties, secrets isolation, controlled promotion between environments, reproducible backfills, data-quality gates, lineage, documented recovery tests, and validated upgrade procedures. The orchestrator helps implement controls; it does not make the pipeline compliant on its own.
